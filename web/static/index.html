            Reading the map

            The map is the repository seen from above. The mainland is the project itself: directories are terraces, files are the buildings standing on them, and a building's height is the number of lines in the file. The islands around it are the package ecosystems the project depends on. Selecting anything draws its dependencies as arcs across the map, with an arrow at the far end of each.

            DragPan the view.
            Right-dragOrbit the view.
            WheelZoom towards the cursor.
            ClickSelect a building and show what it depends on and what depends on it, as arcs over the map.
            Double-clickExpand or collapse a directory or a file; a file expands into its symbols. On open ground it walks you into the map instead.
            Enter / BackspaceExpand or collapse the selection; select its parent.
            Q / ERotate the map by ninety degrees.
            HomeFit the whole map to the window.
            RReset the view: the orientation and zoom the map opened at, fitted to the window.
            + / −Expand or collapse one level throughout.
            /Search files, symbols and packages.
            OOpen the selected file in your editor.
            PSave the map as a PNG image.
            X / KOpen the export menu; save the current view to the configuration file. Both work from the street as well as from the map.
            ?This page. The button at the foot of it shows the introduction again — the map's, or walk mode's if you are out in it.
            BOpen the backpack: everything you have caught, in either view, still open or fixed since. Selecting an entry returns you to where it was caught.
            GOpen the photographs the camera has taken. They are kept for the session only, so the ones worth having are saved from there, one at a time, as PNG files. From the street each one can also be put up on the camera and looked at there.
            Legend clickHide or show a language.
            PinsA marker stands over every building the scanners reported something on, in the color of the worst of them and taller the more there are. Point at one for the tally, or select it to read them.
            The figureWhere you were last standing in walk mode, facing the direction you faced. V puts you back there.

            Walk mode (V)

            Walk mode presents the same map in first person, bent around a small planet. You carry two tools at once, one in each hand: a primary tool for the hunt in your right hand, and a secondary tool that carries you in your left.

            MouseLook around. The pointer is captured at the reticle; Esc releases it and a click on the map captures it again. Where it cannot be captured at all, dragging looks around instead.
            W A S DMove; the arrow keys do the same and also turn. Shift runs, for as long as your wind holds.
            SpaceJump, which costs a little wind. While flying, ascend.
            ClickUse the tool in your right hand. Holding the button down keeps the nail gun and the extinguisher firing; every other tool takes one shot per click.
            F or CUse the tool in your left hand. The middle mouse button does the same. While flying, C descends instead, and F is the throttle.
            RThe tool wheel, which is every tool at once, laid out the way you carry them and numbered the same way the row along the bottom is: the hunt’s row down the right side, what carries you down the left, and an empty left hand at the bottom of it. Hold R, point at one with the mouse and let go; or tap R to leave the wheel up and take what is under the cursor with R again, Enter or a click. Esc or the right button backs out and changes nothing, and so does letting go while the cursor is still in the middle. While it is up you are held where you stand and the city behind it is blurred — nothing moves you, spends a tank or bites you, so changing hands costs no time. Pointing at something already in your hands keeps it — the bare hand at the bottom left is how you put a carried tool down.
            1 2 3Take out a secondary tool, or press the same key again to put it down. Only one is carried at a time.
            4 … 0Take out a primary tool.
            EThe next primary tool, round and round. Your right hand is never empty.
            QThe next secondary tool, and after the last of them an empty left hand — which is how you come down out of the air or step off the water on purpose. Four presses brings you back to where you started.
            HPut both hands away, or take them out again. This is only the drawing of them: both still work, and what they throw then leaves from your eye.
            Hold right buttonLook through the scope. The tracking dart is the tool this is meant for.
            EnterShow the details of whatever the reticle is on, as a second hit would. The pointer is released for reading; a click on the map resumes walking.
            EscHand the mouse back, so the toolbar above the street can be used. You are held where you stand while it is gone — nothing moves you, spends a tank or bites you — and a click on the map takes the mouse back and walks on. The same holds whenever the mouse goes elsewhere, including switching to another tab.
            + / −Change the planet's radius, and so how sharply the world curves. [ and ] do the same.
            V / M / EscReturn to the map. Coming back puts you where you were standing, unless you selected something on the map in the meantime.

            The primary tools

            A primary tool acts on the map. Used on a building it tags the module — the module is selected, its dependency trails light up, and a beacon is planted over it in the color of the worst finding inside it. Using the tool a second time on a tagged building opens its details. Used on a bug it catches it and reads out what the scanner reported. Each tool has a range, and the reticle dims when what you are pointing at is out of range or is not something that tool acts on.

            Fishing rodA long cast that acts on buildings and bugs alike. Where the bobber strikes a wall, the line hauls you to it, which makes the rod a way of getting about as well as a way of tagging. A cast that lands a bug simply lands it.
            Butterfly netSwung rather than thrown, so it has to be walked up to: its range is an arm's length. The stroke goes up over the shoulder and down across the body, and it catches bugs only.
            CameraPhotographs whatever is in the frame, building or bug, at any distance whatsoever. Its back screen shows what its lens is looking at. Every use of it keeps the frame: the picture goes into the photographs (G), captioned with whatever was in it, and the ones worth having are saved from there as files. A second use on a module you have already tagged opens its details, the way every other tool does there, and keeps no picture. A photograph is of the city and of nothing else: neither the camera nor the hand holding it is in it, and neither is anything the interface has drawn on the map — nothing lit, nothing dimmed, no dependency arcs. Any of them can be put back up on that screen from there: the camera comes to hand and is brought right up to your face, square on, until the picture covers nearly the whole view; after a few seconds it goes back down and returns whatever you were carrying. A click puts it away sooner.
            Bubble wandFloats a bubble onto a bug. The bubble leaves slowly, is stopped almost at once by the air, and then rises — so it is aimed early and low, and it barely crosses a street.
            Fire extinguisherHoses foam at bugs at close quarters. Holding the button keeps it discharging, and the foam spreads as it travels and falls out of the air after a few strides. It clears a doorway rather than picking one bug off a roof.
            Tracking dartThe longest and most deliberate shot in the hunt, and the one the scope is for. A dart is lobbed high, falls hard, and steers in the air towards whatever wall lies ahead of it, so a shot thrown over a block still lands. It acts on buildings only, one shot at a time.
            Nail gunThe opposite of the dart in every respect: flat, twice as fast, and effective only across a street. Held down it keeps firing, and a nail leaves a strip nailer crooked, so it hoses a wall rather than picking a spot on it. Anything small enough is pinned to the wall behind it.

            The secondary tools

            A secondary tool tags nothing and catches nothing. It carries you, and carrying it is most of using it. One is held in your left hand alongside whatever your right hand is doing, so the map can be flown over and its bugs caught without putting either down.

            Grapple gunFired with F or C. The hook flies at the building you are looking at and the line pulls you up its facade and onto the roof. From up there, aiming over the edge at anything lower makes the same shot the way down. Space cuts the line.
            Jet backpackCarrying it is flying, for as long as its tank lasts. W and S then move along the direction you are looking — look down and press W to dive — while Space and C go straight up and down. F opens the throttle for a burst of speed. Putting it away is how you come back down.
            Water skimmersCarrying them makes the surface of the water walkable, so the bay becomes a street and you pass beneath the bridges rather than over them. Putting them away over deep water leaves you in it, and so does letting them waterlog.
            FuelThe jet backpack and the water skimmers both run on a tank. It empties only while the tool is doing its work — while the jet is holding you off the ground, while the water is the only thing under the skimmers — and fills again whenever it is not, so neither is a way of getting everywhere. The gauge beside the health bar shows what is left in whichever you are carrying. A tool that runs out stops there and stays stopped: put it away and take it out again once it has filled.

            The hunt

            BugsEvery finding a scanner reported, up to the 140 most severe, walks a lap on the building it belongs to — except a vulnerability proven reachable, which burns as a fire instead — colored by its severity and shaped by it as well: a critical finding is a caterpillar, which crawls and never flies; a high or medium one is a beetle; a low or informational one is a mite. A lap runs around the building at street level, along its facade at some height, across its roof, or as a wider circuit flown around it.
            WindThe second gauge in the corner is what running and jumping are paid for out of. A flat-out sprint empties it in a few seconds and each jump takes a little more; it fills again while you walk or stand still, and once it is gone you have to get a quarter of it back before you can run or jump again. Flying costs none of it — that is the jet's tank rather than your chest.
            HealthThe bar in the corner is what you can take. A fall of more than about ten storeys costs some of it, a bug's bite costs more the worse the finding is, and deep water with nothing to float on takes all of it within a couple of seconds. The bay is not a wall around the map: from a shore it is half a step down, so you can walk into it, wade about in it and climb back out onto any shore. Off a bridge, or off anything else well above the surface, you have to jump in — walking off an edge into a drop is not something you would mean to do. Either way, without the skimmers it is a couple of seconds to reach a shore. Every bug in your backpack raises the bar and mends you by as much. At nothing the screen turns red and the map returns, with everything you caught still in the backpack; walking in again starts at full health.
            TrackerThe sweep in the corner turns with you: one dot per bug still out there, one ring per module already tagged in the color of its worst finding, and an arrow at the rim for anything beyond its range. It tightens as you close on a bug, so the last part of the approach can be made on the sweep rather than by guesswork.
            BackpackWhat you catch is kept, and stays kept across a re-layout, a change of depth and a reload. An entry is retained until the scanners stop reporting it, at which point it is struck through rather than removed.

            Height encodes lines of code. Directories collapse into districts whose footprint grows with the number of files.
